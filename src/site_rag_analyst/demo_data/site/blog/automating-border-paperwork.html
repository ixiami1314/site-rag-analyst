Case study: Van Dijk Logistics cut border paperwork by 63%

Van Dijk Logistics, a Rotterdam freight forwarder handling 400 shipments a week between the EU and the UK, came to us in 2024 with a familiar problem: border clearance paperwork was eating their margins.

The problem

After Brexit, every UK-bound shipment needed customs declarations that operators re-typed from three systems into two portals. Average clearance time was 48 hours, and each re-typing was a chance for a typo that would trigger a hold at the border.

What we built

Over six weeks the team connected their TMS and the customs portals with Flow Connect, cloned the "Customs declaration with approval" template, and tuned it with the mapping language until declarations went out untouched except for a single approval step for shipments above €50,000.

The results

Twelve months on: average clearance time fell from 48 hours to 18 hours — a 63% reduction. Declaration errors dropped 81%. Four operators now handle the same volume two previously struggled with, and SLA breaches for UK lanes fell to under 2% from 14%.

What's next for them

Van Dijk is extending the same pattern to invoice reconciliation with Flow Insights dashboards tracking cycle times per customer. Read more about the products in their stack: Flow Connect and Flow Insights.

Lessons from the migration

Three things made the difference. First, they started with the highest-volume lane only (Rotterdam to Felixstowe) instead of all UK lanes at once — the template was tuned on real volume within two weeks. Second, the approval threshold (shipments above €50,000) was set by their compliance team, not by us; the flow just enforces it. Third, they kept one veteran operator on the exceptions queue from day one, which surfaced the twelve edge cases that mattered before they could become border holds.

What the numbers did not show

The clearance-time drop was expected. The surprise was onboarding: new operators reached full productivity in six days instead of five weeks, because the flow encodes the process — the tribal knowledge lives in version history and approval context cards, not in one person's head.
